Applies immediately after a first call with a prospect. Produces three things in ten minutes: the record, the follow-up, and a qualification call that is honest rather than hopeful.

## The play

1. Do it within ten minutes, before the next call. The specific phrasing a prospect used is gone within the hour, and the phrasing is the most valuable thing the call produced. A debrief written at the end of the day is a summary of a summary.
2. Capture their words first, before any assessment. Three to five verbatim lines: how they described the problem, the number they volunteered, what they have already tried, what they said about timing. What to capture and why verbatim matters is in references/capture-first.md.
3. Then qualify, against the same criteria every time. Fit, problem, authority, timing, and whether there is a real next step with a date. The bar for each, and the rule for what counts as a real next step, are in references/qualification.md. Qualify after capturing, never during, because judging while listening is how a call gets heard as better than it was.
4. Decide the honest category: real, maybe, or not. Most firms have no "not" category, which is why their pipeline is three times its actual size. The categories and what each one means for next steps are in references/qualification.md.
5. Branch by what the call produced:
   - Clear problem, clear owner, a date: write the follow-up and the proposal path.
   - Clear problem, no authority in the room: the next step is access, not a proposal. Write the follow-up to the person who was there, asking who else needs to be in it.
   - Interest with no problem named: not qualified, whatever the enthusiasm. Interest is not a buying signal and is the single most common reason a pipeline is full of nothing.
   - Problem named, no timing, no trigger: real but not now. Record the trigger that would change that, and set a date to check.
   - They talked about price unprompted and early: usually a budget they already have, sometimes a brush-off. The follow-up should ask what it needs to fit inside.
6. Write the follow-up in their language, not the firm's. Quote their phrasing back, name the next step and its date, and keep it short. The structure is in references/the-follow-up.md.
7. Update the record with the verbatim lines, the category and the dated next step. A record with a stage and no next step is a record that will be guessed at in three weeks.
8. Send the follow-up the same day. Next-day follow-ups land into a different context and read as routine.

## What good looks like

- First thing I check that most people skip: whether they named a problem or just expressed interest. Enthusiasm on a call is pleasant and predicts nothing; a specific problem with a cost attached predicts almost everything.
- The mediocre version: a one-line note saying "good call, following up", a stage bumped on optimism, and a follow-up email that restates what the firm does. Three weeks later nobody can remember what the prospect actually wanted.
- I know the debrief is right when the follow-up contains a sentence the prospect would recognise as their own.
- Second test: the category is honest. A debrief where every call comes out as "real" is not a qualification process.
- Red flag: the record has a stage change and no next step with a date. The stage moved because the call felt good.

## Rules

- MUST debrief within ten minutes of the call ending.
- MUST capture verbatim phrasing before writing any assessment.
- MUST qualify against the same criteria every time.
- MUST categorise honestly, including "not", and act on it.
- MUST write the follow-up in their words, with a dated next step.
- MUST record a next step with a date, or no stage change.
- NEVER treat interest with no named problem as qualified.
- NEVER let a call with no decision-maker produce a proposal as its next step.
