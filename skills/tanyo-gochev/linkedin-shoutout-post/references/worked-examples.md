# Worked examples

Three shoutouts I published, what each one did, and what I would change now.

## 1. The numbered breakdown (went to plan)

Subject: a health-tech company's newsletter landing page.

> [Company] has one of the best newsletter landing pages.
>
> Here's what makes it so great:
>
> 1) Headline that's calling out their ICP (healthcare IT executives). This shows who the newsletter is for, and disqualifies the people who aren't a good fit.
>
> 2) A visual showing what the newsletter looks like on their readers' main device (the phone). This helps people see what they are actually signing up for.
>
> 3) Strong social proof, showing logos and testimonials from well-known companies. This adds credibility, which always helps when your audience is full of executives.
>
> If you want to step up your newsletter game, this is a great landing page to study.

Why it worked: one asset, three patterns, each with "what it is" and "why it works". A screenshot of the page went with the post, so every claim was checkable.

What I'd change now: the original ended with "follow me for more content like this". The CTA should point at them. "Give [Company] a follow. This is what good looks like."

## 2. The single pattern (edge case: one strength is enough)

Subject: a founder who runs a free email course.

> [Name] is one of the few people that does this:
>
> As part of his email course, he built a "Thank You" page with a short questionnaire.
>
> Most founders think the work is finished when they get the sign-up. He knows it is just starting.
>
> Asking right after sign-up works because:
> 1) People are most receptive then, so the answers are better.
> 2) You get data to improve your content, and to fit your offers to each segment.
>
> It takes 1 to 2 hours to build. Most founders don't bother. He does.

Why it worked: when someone does one thing that almost nobody does, a single pattern carries the whole post. The "most people don't" contrast does the work that 3 to 5 patterns do in the longer format.

When to use it: the strength is rare and concrete. If you can only find one pattern because you did not research enough, that is not this case. Go back to the checklist.

## 3. The story version (edge case: the shoutout carries your own point of view)

Subject: a marketing leader who publishes long AI workflows free on his newsletter.

> [Name] shows that the most effective way to sell is through education.
>
> Most marketers gate their resources and send 20 sales emails once you opt in. He shares every new workflow in public, on his newsletter, his podcast and LinkedIn. Long, dense, complicated workflows.
>
> Most founders are afraid to send long emails because "we don't want to bother our audience". You're only bothering your audience if your content isn't great.
>
> I just became a paid subscriber without receiving a single sales email from him.

Why it worked: the last line is the proof. A real action you took because of their work is stronger evidence than any metric.

The risk: it leans on your opinion ("a hill I'm willing to die on"). That is fine as long as the subject is the hero of the post, not your opinion. If you could delete their name and the post still stands, it has turned into a hot take. Rewrite it.

## A format that is not a shoutout

A "people I recommend" roundup (12 names, one line each) is useful and gets tags, but it is a referral list, not a shoutout. There is no breakdown, so it teaches nothing and does not show judgment. Post it separately; do not count it as your weekly shoutout.
