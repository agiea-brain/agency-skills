# Worked cases

Three real audits. Client names and identifying details are removed, and the numbers are real.

## Went to plan: the flagship lost

A solo B2B content consultant scored four clients. By invoice, the best client was a $7,500-a-month retainer with a large company. It took about 12 hours a week, about $145 an hour against the $227 the income goal needed, and half of the program it paid for sat idle behind the client's internal silos. Once Pay reflected the hours, the flagship dropped to the bottom half of the roster. Two short agency projects paid $417 and $583 an hour, but neither founder published the content that got delivered, so Proof was dead and the work drained the consultant.

The winner was a $5,000 one-off course: about 20 hours of work, around $60,000 in revenue the client traced back to it, and 10 out of 12 on the scores. The consultant named the pattern in the session: the work that energized them was content tied to revenue. The seed became measurable results instead of a segment, so the agency-or-enterprise question the consultant walked in with could wait.

## Edge: the cash cow wasn't the pattern

An advisor to agencies scored three clients live. My read going in had the easiest client as the one to clone. It had taken a doubled rate with no pushback and scored +3 on Pay and +3 on Energy. Scoring it in the session, the owner gave it Fit -1: it paid well and asked little, but nothing about it repeated. It funds the business; it doesn't shape the offer. A second client scored +3 on Energy and Proof but was too small and the wrong kind of firm, so it also got Fit -1.

The owner picked the client with the lowest total: -1 on Pay and -1 on Energy, with +3 on Proof and +3 on Fit. Two negatives would normally send it to the exit list. Both came from the owner over-investing in the engagement, and a priced, scoped offer fixes both. The seed became that kind of firm, a full-service agency of about $2.5M losing revenue on an outdated model, and not that engagement's terms.

## The trap: raising the rate didn't fix the rate

A solo consultant in a niche industry had already raised prices hard within a year: project fees nearly tripled and retainers doubled. The book still ran $87-$300 an hour. An income goal of $200,000 at 20 hours a week needed $185-$333 an hour, depending on how many of those hours were billable, so the floor became $250, the rate the consultant already charged hourly clients.

The leak was scoping and terms: back halves tied to client launches the consultant didn't control, work that was hard to scope, and one engagement not yet billed at all. So the fixes were all terms: charge upfront, never tie payment to a launch someone else controls, and sell advisory as a package instead of by the hour.
