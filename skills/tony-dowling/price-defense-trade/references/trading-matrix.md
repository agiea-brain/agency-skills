# Diagnosis signals and the trading matrix

## Reading the pushback

| Cause | What the notes and record usually show | What it is really asking |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Genuine budget constraint | A named budget figure, a finance sign-off step, a smaller scope raised by the buyer, a start date tied to a new financial year | "Help me buy this inside a number I cannot move" |
| (b) Competitive pressure | A rival named, a like-for-like comparison, "they quoted", a sudden request for a line-item breakdown | "Tell me why you are worth the difference" |
| (c) Procurement testing nerve | A new contact late in the deal, a request for "your best price" with no reason, a template negotiation email, the buyer still enthusiastic | "Show me you believe your own price" |

State the diagnosis as one line: cause, then the two or three pieces of evidence, then what it means for the approach.

## Part 1: Value Re-Anchor

Structure, in the rep's own voice:

1. Restate the problem the prospect described, in their words from the notes.
2. Put their number on it: what the problem costs them a month, or what the risk of inaction is (a fine, a lost contract, a delayed launch).
3. Set the price beside that number.
4. Stop talking.

The anchor is their figure, not the firm's. If the notes do not hold a figure, the first move in the meeting is to get one, not to defend price.

## Part 2: Concession Trading Matrix

Three trades, each only if the prospect insists on movement. Every trade gives something the Menu for Sale allows and gets something of equal or greater commercial value.

| Give | Get | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| A percentage off | Annual payment upfront instead of quarterly or monthly | Cash-flow matters to the firm; cause (a) or (c) |
| A percentage off | A 24-month term instead of 12 | The relationship is worth more than the margin; cause (b) |
| A percentage off | Named logo rights, a written case study, two referral introductions | The prospect is a credible name in a target sector; any cause |
| A smaller scope: fewer deliverables, lower frequency, one site instead of three | The prospect's number, at the same rate per unit | Cause (a), when the budget is real and fixed |
| A phased start | A signed agreement for the full scope today, phase two dated | Cause (a), when the constraint is this quarter only |
| Faster onboarding or a senior lead on the account | Signature this week | Cause (c), when procurement is stalling a buyer who has already decided |

Rules for the matrix:

- Every row names a specific deliverable or term from the Menu for Sale. "Reduced scope" is not a trade until the deliverable being removed is named.
- Never two discounts in one matrix. One discount row at most; the others move scope, term, timing or reference value.
- Write each row as a sentence the rep can say: "If the annual payment works for you, I can do [X]. Otherwise the standard terms stand."

## Part 3: Walk-Away / Class Line

The line holds status. It is not a threat and not an apology. It says, in effect: this is what it costs to solve the problem you described, and the firm would rather not solve it badly.

Shape: acknowledge their position, restate what the price buys in one clause, state what the firm will not do, leave the door open.

Pattern: "I understand the number you need to hit. What I will not do is take deliverables out quietly and hand you a thinner version of the thing you asked for. If [the trade] works, there is a route. If not, I would rather you went with someone else than went with us and got less than you need."

Avoid: "this is our final offer", "I'll see what I can do", "I'm not able to go any lower", anything that invites one more round.

## Takeaway document

One page. Diagnosis line at the top. Three sections with the scripts in quotes. The matrix as a table with the three chosen trades only. A footer line: "Terms verified against [Menu for Sale document name]" or "Terms unverified: check against the client's Menu for Sale before the meeting."
