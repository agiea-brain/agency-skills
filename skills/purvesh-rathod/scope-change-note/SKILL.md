Applies the moment an out-of-scope request arrives, before anyone starts the work. Produces a decision on how to treat it and the message that goes back to the client.

## The play

1. Check what was actually sold. Read the scope and the exclusions in the signed document, not the memory of the sales conversation. Half of all scope arguments are a request that was always in scope and nobody checked.

2. Price it in hours before deciding anything, including the review and revision rounds it drags with it. A request that feels like twenty minutes is usually three hours once someone senior has looked at it twice. Pricing it first makes the next decision a commercial one rather than a mood.

3. Sort the request:
   - Already in scope: do it, say nothing about scope, and stop. Arguing about something the client is entitled to costs more than the work.
   - Small, one-off, and the relationship is healthy: absorb it and say so explicitly. Goodwill works only when it is visible. Silent absorption teaches the client it was always free.
   - Small but it will recur: this is the dangerous one, and it is where most agency margin goes. Treat the first instance as the moment to name the pattern, not the fifth.
   - Large, or it displaces committed work: a change order. Say what it costs and what it moves.
   - A trade: take it on in exchange for something the firm wants. More runway on a deadline, a case study, an introduction, a longer term.

4. Say what it displaces. Everything displaces something, and a client who hears "yes, and it pushes the landing page to the 14th" makes an informed choice. A client who hears only yes assumes capacity is infinite and asks again next week.

5. Write the note. Lead with the yes or the willingness, then the cost, then the choice. Never lead with the constraint, and never present it as a complaint about the request.

6. If the answer is a change order, make it one page: what is being added, what it costs, what date moves, and a line to approve. A change order that requires a new contract does not get signed.

7. Log it. The pattern across a quarter is the real finding, and it is what tells the firm whether this client is underpriced or this service line is underscoped for everyone.

## Rules

- MUST read the signed scope and exclusions before deciding anything.
- MUST price the request in hours, including review and revisions, before responding.
- MUST name the pattern on the first recurring request, not the fifth.
- MUST state what the work displaces.
- MUST make goodwill visible when absorbing something.
- MUST keep a change order to one page with a line to approve.
- NEVER start out-of-scope work before the decision is made.
- NEVER lead the message with the constraint or with the cost.
- NEVER absorb a request silently and call it a relationship investment.
