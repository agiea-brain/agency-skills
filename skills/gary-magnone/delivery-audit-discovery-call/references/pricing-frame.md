# Pricing frame and objection handling

Pricing happens on the call. Stall rate roughly doubles when pricing goes by email, because the owner does the value math alone and without the anchor.

## When to open it

Wait for the owner to ask. If they have not asked by minute 24 (existing customer) or minute 27 (net new):

- "Want me to share what the investment usually looks like?"

## The frame, in order

1. **Restate their figures.** "Based on the value you just described, [their pain] is costing you about [their figure] a year, plus the opportunity cost of [what they'd do]."
2. **Anchor at the standard range.** "Most customers in similar situations land between [standard low] and [standard high] a month."
3. **Offer the early-partner rate for this scope.** "That said, we're early on this offer and being deliberate about who we work with. Looking for co-partners more than customers. So for you, based on this scope, I'd propose we start at [partner rate] a month. As scope expands, we revisit."
4. **Check the ballpark.** "Does that feel like the right ballpark for the value we just talked about?"
5. **Describe the build in one breath.** "What we'd do is embed an engineer with you for the next few weeks to build a custom solution for [the pain]. Build is included. After deployment, we keep it running, maintain it, improve it. First [scoping period] are no-charge while we onboard and scope."

Then stop talking.

The figures the script shipped with, for a custom AI-build offer to service firms: standard range $5K to $7K a month, early-partner rate $3K to $4K a month, scoping period 10 days. Substitute your own; keep the structure. The anchor must be the standard range, and the partner rate must be tied to the scope they just described, so the discount reads as selectivity rather than weakness.

A lower-ticket pilot variant for an existing customer: a fixed one-month pilot fee, refunded in full if the agreed hours are not saved, rolling into an annual license if they are. State the hours target out loud; it comes from the pain math.

## Handling the "is this worth it" pause

Go back to their number, not yours:

- "You mentioned this was costing you roughly [their figure] a year. If this solves it, even partially, does the math work for you?"

This is not pressure. It is the calculation they are already doing in their head, done out loud.

## Objections

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "Price is too high" | "Fair. What were you expecting, and what would make it a no-brainer?" |
| "Need to check with my partner" | "Totally get it. Would it help if I did a quick 15-minute walkthrough with them? Way more effective than summarizing secondhand." |
| "Need to think about it" | "Totally fair. What specifically are you weighing?" Then send the recap tonight and book 15 minutes to either kick off or close the loop. |
| "We've tried tools for this" | "What did you try, and why didn't it stick?" Usually the answer is the manual cleanup step, which is the step you are proposing to build for. |
| Silence after the price | Ask the ballpark question again. Silence is not a no. |

## Isolating price

If the only objection is price, say so and test it:

- "All things remaining the same, if price wasn't a question, do you have any other hesitations?"

If no: "Cool. What would make sense?" You want a value conversation where it is a no-brainer for them; if it is not, it does not make sense for either side.

If yes: that hesitation is the real objection. Handle it first; price was a proxy.
